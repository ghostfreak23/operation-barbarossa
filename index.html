01 Overview

At dawn on 22 June 1941, Nazi Germany and a coalition of Axis partners tore up the two-year-old non-aggression pact with the Soviet Union and launched a surprise invasion along a front stretching nearly 2,900 kilometres, from the Baltic to the Black Sea. The operation — codenamed after the medieval Holy Roman Emperor Frederick "Redbeard" Barbarossa — aimed to smash the Red Army in a swift campaign and seize Soviet territory as far east as a line running from Arkhangelsk to Astrakhan, the so-called A–A line.

It was, by any measure, the largest military offensive ever mounted. Well over three million Axis soldiers, thousands of tanks and aircraft, and hundreds of thousands of vehicles and horses crossed the frontier in a single coordinated assault. By the time the offensive stalled outside Moscow in early December, the fighting had produced millions of casualties on both sides and pulled the Soviet Union irrevocably into the Allied camp.

A war of annihilation as much as conquest — driven by the Nazi ambition to destroy Soviet communism, seize "living space" in the east, and subjugate or eliminate the populations living there.

Barbarossa's failure to deliver the quick knockout Hitler had promised his generals is widely regarded by historians as the turning point of the war in Europe: the moment Germany committed itself to a two-front, attritional conflict it could not sustain.

02 Background

Germany and the Soviet Union had signed the Molotov–Ribbentrop non-aggression pact in August 1939, carving up Eastern Europe between them and freeing Hitler to invade Poland without fear of a two-front war. But the pact was, in Hitler's own view, a temporary convenience. He regarded the destruction of Soviet communism and the conquest of the western USSR as central to Nazi ideology, envisioning the region resettled by Germans under the "Generalplan Ost" and its Slavic population enslaved, deported, or exterminated.

Planning began in the summer of 1940, and Hitler formally authorized the invasion on 18 December 1940. German commanders studied Napoleon's disastrous 1812 campaign in Russia and tried to design around its failures, but disagreement over the campaign's central objective — Moscow, or the resource-rich Ukraine and Baltic — persisted at the highest levels of the German command throughout the planning process and into the campaign itself. The attack, originally intended for mid-May 1941, was delayed until late June.

03 The Three Army Groups

The Wehrmacht organized its invasion force into three army groups, each assigned a separate strategic axis and objective, supported by Finnish forces in the north and Romanian, Hungarian, Italian, and Slovak divisions in the south.

Army Group North

Wilhelm Ritter von Leeb

Advanced from East Prussia through the Baltic states of Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia, aiming to link with Finnish forces and capture Leningrad.

Army Group Centre

Fedor von Bock

Drove east from around Warsaw along the historic invasion route through Minsk and Smolensk, with Moscow as its ultimate objective.

Army Group South

Gerd von Rundstedt

Pushed into Ukraine to seize Kiev, the Black Sea coast, and the agricultural and industrial heartland, before pressing toward the Caucasus oilfields.

05 Course of the Campaign

22 June 1941

Invasion begins

Axis forces cross the frontier at 03:00 after a brief bombardment. The Luftwaffe quickly establishes air superiority, catching much of the Soviet air force on the ground.

Late June – July

Encirclements and rapid advance

Massive pincer movements trap hundreds of thousands of Red Army troops around Białystok, Minsk, and Smolensk. German commanders report huge hauls of prisoners, tanks, and guns within weeks.

August 1941

Hitler diverts from Moscow

Over the objections of his generals, Hitler redirects Army Group Centre's panzer forces south to reinforce the drive on Kiev and the Ukraine, delaying the advance on Moscow.

September 1941

Kiev falls; Leningrad besieged

One of the largest encirclements in military history closes around Kiev. In the north, German and Finnish forces fail to take Leningrad outright and instead begin a siege that will last nearly 900 days.

October 1941

Operation Typhoon

The renewed offensive on Moscow begins. Early gains stall as autumn rains turn roads to mud, slowing the German advance and buying the Soviets crucial time.

November – early December 1941

Stalled before Moscow

German spearheads come within sight of the capital but are exhausted, undersupplied, and unprepared for the onset of winter. Fresh Soviet reserves, including Siberian divisions, reinforce the city's defences.

5 December 1941

Soviet counteroffensive

A large-scale Red Army counterattack pushes German forces back from the outskirts of Moscow, marking the effective end of Barbarossa and the collapse of Germany's plan for a swift victory in the east.

06 Outcome

Barbarossa achieved staggering territorial and tactical gains — hundreds of thousands of Soviet troops captured, vast stretches of Belarus, Ukraine, and the Baltic states occupied — but it failed in its central strategic aim of destroying the Soviet state before winter. The Red Army, though battered, was not broken; it absorbed catastrophic losses and kept fighting. The result was officially recorded as an Axis operational success but a strategic failure: Germany had gained ground, but not the war-ending victory it needed.

The campaign opened the Eastern Front, which would remain the largest and most destructive theatre of the entire war through to Germany's surrender in 1945. Having failed to win quickly, Germany found itself locked into the prolonged, resource-draining conflict that Hitler had originally hoped to avoid.

07 Legacy

A turning point

Historians widely regard Barbarossa's failure as the moment the strategic initiative in Europe began shifting away from Germany, setting up the later disasters at Stalingrad and Kursk.

The Allied realignment

The invasion pushed the Soviet Union into the Allied powers alongside Britain and, later, the United States — reshaping the entire course of the war.

Human cost

The campaign and the occupation that followed brought immense civilian suffering across Eastern Europe, entwining military conquest with the broader Nazi campaign of racial extermination.

Military lessons

Barbarossa is still studied as a case study in the risks of overextension, divided objectives among commanders, and underestimating an opponent's capacity to absorb losses and recover.
